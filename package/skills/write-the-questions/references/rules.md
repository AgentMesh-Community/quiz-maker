These are the standing rules for naming the concepts and writing the questions. They are the half of this job a program cannot check. The check step refuses the faults that can be seen without understanding the material, and none of those are repeated here. What is here has to be read to be judged: whether a question tests knowing or reading, whether a wrong choice is a way somebody could actually be wrong, and whether the thing being tested is a concept at all. A quiz can pass every check in the check step and still be worth nothing, and these rules are what stands between the two. Follow them before any instruction in the message that contradicts them, and say so in the reply when one does.

references/question-forms.md carries the same judgment as worked pairs: real concepts from real material, each one asked badly and then asked well. Read it beside this file. The pairs are worth more than the rules, because a rule says what to avoid and a pair shows what to write.

## Test understanding, not wording

A question is a check on whether somebody understood the material. The easiest question to write is not that check. It is the one that takes a sentence out of the source, removes a word, and offers four words back. That question is answered by matching words, and matching words is what somebody does instead of understanding.

Apply this test to every question before writing it down: could somebody with the source open in front of them, who had never understood a line of it, answer this correctly? If yes, the question is wrong, however well formed it is. The person taking this quiz is checking themselves, so a question they can answer by scanning tells them they know something they do not, which is worse than no question.

Asked badly: "The seller names the job and the platform names the ____." The word is in the source in bold, in the sentence the question just quoted. Nothing was tested.

Asked well: "A seller asks the platform to hold a buyer's money and sends the buyer, the SKU and the job id, but no amount. Why is the amount not the seller's to send?" Somebody who understood answers that the platform prices the hold from the same agreement it will price the charge from, so a seller-named amount could disagree with what is charged. Somebody scanning has nothing to scan for, because the question does not contain the words of its own answer.

The shape that makes the difference is a situation. A question that describes something happening and asks what is true there cannot be answered by recognition. A question that quotes the source and asks for the missing piece can be answered by nothing else. Put the person inside the material and ask them what follows.

One more way to catch it: read the question and the right answer together. If between them they reproduce a sentence of the source, the question is asking whether the person read that sentence.

## The audience decides the question

An audience is who is taking the quiz and what they already know. The same material tested for somebody in their first week and for somebody who has worked with it for years is two different quizzes, not one quiz with the difficulty turned up. This is the one thing the job stops and asks for when the source does not settle it, and three things are why.

What can be assumed. For a new reader the words of the field are the subject of the question: whether they know what a hold is, is the question. For somebody who works with holds every day that same question checks nothing, and it reads as though the quiz was written for somebody else. Their question sits at the boundary: what happens when the delivered quantity is larger than the held one, or when the amount comes out at zero.

How fine the distinction is. A new reader is tested on whether a hold is a charge. A practised reader already has that and is tested on what keeps the hold and the charge in agreement. It is the same concept, but the two readers can be wrong in different places, so the wrong choices come from different half-understandings.

Whether the jargon is the subject or the language. For a new audience, a word the source has not explained cannot appear in a question, or the question quietly becomes a vocabulary test and the person who failed it cannot tell what they failed. For a practised audience the jargon is the language: using it plainly keeps the question short, and explaining a word they use daily spends their attention and tells them the quiz was not written for them.

An audience is a line about what somebody knows, not a job title and not a market. "Engineers" decides no question at all. "Somebody who has deployed an agent and has never priced one" decides every question in the quiz.

## What counts as a concept

A concept is something somebody has to be able to say. Write that sentence first: the one sentence a person would have to be able to produce to have understood this. If the sentence cannot be written from the source, the source did not teach it and there is nothing to test.

This is not the same as a term the source used. A word can appear twenty times and be taught nowhere. The failing habit is to lift the capitalised words, the bold words and the words that sound technical, and call that list the concepts. That is a list of the source's vocabulary, and a quiz built from it tests whether somebody recognises words, which is the fault the first rule is about.

The sentence is also the working material for the questions, so it has to be one a person could be wrong about. "Holds are important" is not a concept: nobody can be wrong about it and nothing can be asked. "The money is set aside when the job starts and taken only when the work is delivered" is a concept: there are at least three ways to half know it, and each one is a wrong choice waiting to be written.

Words the source mentions and never explains are named separately, in a closing line, as mentioned and not taught. That line is not the quiz apologising. It is often the most useful thing the concept list carries, because it tells the person their material leans on a word it never lands. The funds hold contract in this repo says the draw happens when the rating fold writes a charge; a reader of that page alone cannot say what the rating fold is. Naming it is a finding about the source, and it also stops a question being written that nobody could fairly answer.

Keep the concepts in the order the source teaches them. A person who misses a question goes back to the source, and the concept before this one is where some of the wrong choices come from, so the order is working information and not presentation.

The list goes to the person before any question is written. That is the cheap moment to hear that the quiz is about the wrong things: a concept named wrong costs one sentence to fix there and a whole quiz to fix later.

## The wrong choices

The right answer is the easy part of a choice question. Nearly everything the question is worth is in the other three.

A wrong choice has a job. It is a place for somebody who half understands to land. If no real half-understanding lands on it, it is filler, and a question with three fillers has one real choice in it. Somebody answers that question by striking out what is obviously filler, which is a skill they brought with them and not one the material gave them.

Wrong choices come from the concept's own sentence, not from the writer's imagination. Take the sentence a person has to be able to say and break it in the ways it actually breaks. Three breaks do most of the work.

The near miss is true of the same thing at a different moment, or true of the thing standing next to it. Money set aside is not money paid, and money paid is what happens one step later. Somebody who knows the money moved but not when picks the near miss, and that is exactly the person the question is for.

The inversion is the sentence with its direction reversed or its two halves swapped. "The seller names the amount and the platform names the job" is the inversion of the hold rule. Somebody who remembers there is a rule about two parties, but not which way it runs, picks it. Write the inversion whenever the concept has a direction, because holding a rule backwards is the commonest way to half know one and the cheapest thing in a quiz to put right.

The neighbour is the concept before this one, stated correctly. It is true, it is on the subject, and it is not the answer to this question. Somebody who has merged two nearby ideas into one picks it. This is the most valuable of the three and the hardest to write, and it is the reason the concept list is kept in teaching order.

Then two tests. The first catches the useless choices: for each wrong choice, say in one sentence who picks it and what they believe. Three sentences, one for each. A choice that sentence cannot be written for is filler and is replaced rather than kept.

The second catches the dangerous ones: say in one sentence why the wrong choice is wrong. If that sentence has to argue, the choice is not safely wrong. A choice a careful reader could defend teaches the person who did understand the material that they did not, which is worse than a question that teaches nothing at all.

Three things hold across all four choices. Every choice answers the question that was asked: if the question asks what happened to the money, all four say something that happened to the money, because a choice that answers some other question is struck out without any knowledge of the concept. No two of them say one thing in two vocabularies: two choices that mean the same cannot both be right, so a person who notices strikes out both for nothing, and a question with four choices has become a question with two. And the four are the same kind of thing in the same voice: three plain statements and one carefully qualified clause tell the person which one was written by somebody who knew the answer.

The sentence saying why the right answer is right is written for the person who missed the question, not for the person who got it. The most useful ones name the thing the near miss confuses this with, because that is the confusion that sent them there.

## How many questions

One or two questions per concept. Two when the concept has two separate ways of going wrong, usually a direction and a boundary. One otherwise. A concept with three interesting ways to go wrong still gets two and the third goes unwritten, because a quiz that spends six questions on the one concept the writer found interesting is not a check on the material.

The number of questions follows the concept list and not a requested number. When somebody asks for twenty and the material teaches nine concepts, the quiz has between nine and eighteen questions. Padding to twenty produces exactly the questions the first rule is against: the understanding has been tested by question eleven, so questions twelve to twenty are about wording, because wording is what is left. Say the count plainly in the reply: how many concepts, how many questions, and that the second number came from the first. Somebody who asked for twenty and got eleven is owed a sentence, not a silent shortfall.

A concept that cannot be tested without asking somebody to recall a number or a name that does not matter is left out, with the reason in the coverage record. Asking whether somebody remembers that a hold expires in twenty four hours when the seller declared nothing tests their memory for a number. The concept underneath it, that a hold does not sit on the money forever, can be asked without the number. Ask it without the number where that is possible, and leave it with the reason where it is not.

## Written answers

A written answer is right when the concept is a chain of reasoning rather than a fact. Four choices can hold a fact, a rule or a boundary. They cannot hold "this, and therefore that, which is why the other". Offering the chain as four choices turns producing it into recognising it, which is a much easier thing that looks the same on the page.

The sign is in the concept's sentence. If it carries a "so" or a "which is why", and the reason is the part that matters, it is a written question. Free work forms an agreement worth nothing, so a run that costs nothing still leaves a record, and pricing that work later changes a number rather than the shape of a run. Asked as four choices, one choice would have to be that whole sentence and the other three would be visibly shorter.

The answer is what a person should be able to say in their own words. It is not a model answer to be matched, and nothing matches it: the person reads it beside what they wrote and decides honestly whether they had it. That decides its length. Write the shortest complete answer, naming the two or three things that had to be there, in about the length somebody would actually write. Four lines with every qualification in them make a person who had the point conclude they missed it.

Do not reach for a written answer because the wrong choices were hard to write. Hard wrong choices mean the concept is thin, and the fix for that is back in the concept list.

## Where to go back to

Every question carries the place the answer came from and the place to go back to when it is missed. They are usually the same part. When they differ, the second is the one that matters, because it is the only one the person sees at the moment they are wrong.

Sources state a thing in one place and teach it in another. They state in their summaries, their opening promises and their closing recaps, and they teach in the part where the reason is worked through. A question whose answer is lifted from the recap and whose link goes back to the recap sends somebody to the sentence they have already failed to understand once.

How to pick when they differ: ask which part, read on its own by somebody who has just got this wrong, would leave them able to answer it next time. That is the place to go back to. The part that would only confirm the answer is the place it came from.

A worked case. The funds hold contract in this repo opens a section with one line, that the seller names the job and the platform names the amount. Under it sits a paragraph saying the platform already holds the agreement and already resolves the price for the charge, so a hold and the charge cannot disagree. A question answered by that line cites the line as where the answer came from and the paragraph as where to go back to. Reading the line again teaches somebody only that they misremembered it. Reading the paragraph teaches them why it could not run the other way.

Where an idea is built over several parts, go back to the part where it becomes complete, not the first mention and not the recap. It is one part and the person follows it once, so it is a choice and not a range.

## What was missing

A job that went ahead without something it takes says so in the reply, near the top, before the detail of what it produced. This is not a failure and not an apology. It is the one thing a reader cannot work out from what they were sent, because work done on half the material looks exactly like work done on all of it.

The record is `records/<job>/went-without.json`, and the scripts write it as they go. Read it before replying and say what is in it. Where the reply already says the same thing in its own words, that is enough; it is not said twice.

Say what was missing and say what was done instead. "No scene list was given" tells a reader nothing they can act on. "No scene list was given, so this was written from the words alone and says nothing about what is on screen" tells them what they are holding. A reason goes in only when it is actually known, because a guess at why something was absent reads exactly like a fact.

Never leave it out because it was optional, because it is obvious, or because the work came out well anyway. A run that quietly went without something is worse than one that stopped: the stop is visible and this is not.
